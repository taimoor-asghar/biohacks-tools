How caffeine half-life works

Caffeine does not fade from your body in a straight line. It follows exponential decay, which means the same fraction disappears every hour, regardless of how much is left. The half-life is simply the time it takes for that fraction to reach one half: if you start with 200 mg and your half-life is 5 hours, you have 100 mg after 5 hours, 50 mg after 10 hours, and 25 mg after 15 hours. Each half-life cuts the remainder in half again.

For most healthy adults the caffeine half-life falls between 3 and 7 hours, with around 5 hours as a commonly cited average. The math is a single formula: remaining = dose × 0.5(elapsed hours / half-life). After two half-lives (10 hours at a 5-hour half-life), about a quarter of the original dose is still circulating. After three half-lives (15 hours), roughly one eighth remains. This is why an afternoon coffee can still be a meaningful dose at midnight even when you no longer feel wired — feeling awake and being caffeine-free are not the same thing.

One important detail: tolerance changes how you feel, not how fast you clear. A person who drinks four cups a day metabolizes caffeine at roughly the same rate as an occasional drinker with similar genetics. The daily drinker simply notices the stimulation less, because the brain adapts by growing extra adenosine receptors. The caffeine is still there, still occupying receptors, still nudging sleep architecture in the wrong direction. This is the single most misunderstood fact about caffeine timing, and it is exactly what this timer corrects for: it tracks the molecule, not the sensation.

Why 50 mg is the sleep threshold

This timer flags the moment your remaining caffeine drops below 50 mg — roughly half a small brewed coffee, or one strong cup of black tea. That number is a practical rule of thumb, not a biological law. There is no magic concentration at which caffeine suddenly stops affecting sleep; the relationship is dose-dependent, which is why a conservative cutoff is more useful than a precise one.

The evidence behind conservative timing is solid. A widely cited study by Drake and colleagues (2013) found that 400 mg of caffeine taken 6 hours before bed still reduced total sleep by more than an hour, and even smaller evening doses have been shown to delay sleep onset and reduce deep sleep in sensitive people. Most sleep clinicians use 50 mg as a pragmatic line: below it, the large majority of people sleep normally. Highly sensitive individuals — and anyone struggling with insomnia — may do better with an even lower personal cutoff, which is why the timer also shows your raw milligram number: use it to learn your own line.

Worth knowing: caffeine does not only make it harder to fall asleep. Measurable blood levels at bedtime are associated with less deep (slow-wave) sleep and more fragmented sleep — more micro-awakenings you may not remember, but which leave you less refreshed. People often blame "stress" for unrefreshing sleep when a 4 pm coffee is the actual culprit, because by midnight they felt fine and never connected the two.

What changes your half-life

The headline average of 5 hours hides enormous individual variation, and most of it is not your fault or your virtue — it is genetics and physiology.

CYP1A2 genetics. The liver enzyme CYP1A2 does almost all caffeine clearance, and common variants of the gene split people into fast and slow metabolizers. Fast metabolizers can halve caffeine in about 3 hours; slow metabolizers may need 6 to 7. This is why one person can drink espresso after dinner and sleep like a stone while their partner is still staring at the ceiling. If you do not know which you are, the slider default of 5 hours is a reasonable middle, and you can calibrate it against your own experience.

Pregnancy. Caffeine half-life lengthens dramatically in pregnancy — up to 10 to 15 hours in the third trimester — because the same enzyme system slows down. Most guidelines advise keeping total intake under about 200 mg per day during pregnancy, and the timer's math still applies if you stretch the half-life slider (use the slowest setting as a proxy, and discuss caffeine habits with your obstetrician).

Smoking and oral contraceptives. These push in opposite directions. Smoking induces CYP1A2 and can shorten the half-life to around 3 to 4 hours. Combined oral contraceptives inhibit the enzyme and can roughly double the half-life. Both matter more than most people realize: starting or stopping the pill can effectively change your caffeine tolerance overnight.

Age, liver function, and medications. Clearance slows with age and with liver disease. A handful of medications interact with CYP1A2 — fluvoxamine is the classic example, and some antibiotics in the quinolone family can slow caffeine clearance too. If you take regular medication and notice new caffeine sensitivity, that is a conversation for your pharmacist or doctor, not a slider adjustment.

What does not change it much. Drinking water, exercising, sweating, and eating a big meal do not meaningfully speed caffeine metabolism. The enzyme works at its own pace. Once the caffeine is absorbed — typically within 45 minutes — time is the only real remedy.

Practical curfew rules

The timer gives you a personalized caffeine curfew: the latest clock time your last dose should happen so that you are below 50 mg by bedtime, rounded down to the nearest 30 minutes for a safety margin. Rounding down matters, because every input here is approximate — the stated caffeine content of your drink, your true half-life, the exact minute you finished the cup.

As a starting point for people who do not want to do math: the popular 8 to 10 hour rule — no caffeine within 8 to 10 hours of bedtime — exists because it keeps even slow metabolizers under the threshold for a normal-strength coffee. If you sleep at 23:00, that means a last coffee around 13:00 to 15:00. The timer personalizes this: fast metabolizers with a light morning dose get a later curfew, slow metabolizers with a double espresso get an earlier one.

Three habits make the curfew easier to keep. First, front-load: get your caffeine in the first half of the morning, when it also does the most for alertness, since adenosine has been building since you woke. Second, taper the afternoon: if you need a ritual at 15:00, make it half-caf or green tea rather than a full brew. Third, learn your personal number from the timer output: if you consistently sleep well with 80 mg at bedtime but badly with 120 mg, your true threshold is somewhere between, and you can stop worshipping the default 50.

The evidence — and its limits

The core pharmacokinetics here are not controversial: caffeine follows first-order (exponential) elimination, and the 3-to-7-hour half-life range comes from decades of blood-concentration studies. The 50 mg cutoff, by contrast, is a heuristic — defensible, widely used, but not a finding from a randomized trial. No ethics board is going to randomize people to precise bedtime caffeine concentrations, so a pragmatic line is the best anyone can offer.

This timer also simplifies reality in ways you should know about. It models a single dose as one smooth exponential curve, but real clearance has an absorption phase (levels rise for 30 to 60 minutes after drinking) and can deviate from a perfect curve at very high doses. It assumes your half-life is constant through the day; in practice, clearance can be slightly slower in the evening. And it cannot know your genetics, your pill, or your pregnancy — those live in the slider, and in your judgment.

None of this makes the tool useless. A simplified model with honest inputs beats confident guesswork, and most people's caffeine problems come not from pharmacokinetic subtleties but from massively underestimating what is still in their blood at midnight. Use the timer to build intuition for a week, then you will rarely need it: you will simply know what a 15:00 espresso costs you at 23:00.

If caffeine is tangled up with anxiety, heart palpitations, pregnancy, or a sleep disorder that is not improving, the right next step is your doctor — not a finer slider setting. This page is educational, not medical advice.

Frequently asked questions

Does decaf coffee count toward my caffeine total?

Yes, but only a little. A typical cup of decaf still contains roughly 2 to 7 mg of caffeine depending on the bean and brew method. For most people that amount is negligible, but if you are very caffeine-sensitive or you drink several cups of decaf in the evening, it can add up. Include it as a small custom dose in the timer if you want to be precise.

Why am I more sensitive to caffeine than my friend?

Most of the difference comes down to genetics, specifically the CYP1A2 gene, which controls the liver enzyme that breaks caffeine down. "Fast metabolizers" can clear caffeine in about 3 hours, while "slow metabolizers" may need 6 to 7 hours or more. Sensitivity to caffeine's effects is partly separate and involves adenosine receptor differences. If your friend falls asleep easily after an evening espresso and you cannot, genetics is the most likely explanation.

Does building a tolerance change my caffeine half-life?

Not much. Tolerance changes how strongly you feel caffeine's effects, because your brain grows extra adenosine receptors to compensate. The speed at which your liver clears caffeine from the blood stays roughly the same. This means a heavy daily coffee drinker still has caffeine circulating late into the evening even if they no longer feel wired from it, and it can still fragment their sleep.

How accurate is the 50 mg sleep threshold?

It is a practical rule of thumb, not a biological law. Research has shown that 200 mg taken even 6 hours before bed can measurably disrupt sleep, while smaller amounts have mixed effects. Fifty milligrams, roughly half a small brewed coffee, is a conservative line that most sleep practitioners use: below it, the majority of people sleep normally. Sensitive individuals may need a lower cutoff, which is why the timer's personal curfew rounds conservatively downward.

Can I clear caffeine from my system faster?

Not in any meaningful way. Drinking water, exercising, or eating will not significantly speed up caffeine metabolism, because clearance is driven by a liver enzyme working at its own genetically set pace. Smoking speeds it up and oral contraceptives slow it down, but neither is a practical or advisable strategy. Once the caffeine is in you, the only real remedy is time.

Should I count the caffeine in tea, chocolate, and pre-workout?

Yes. A cup of black tea has about 40 to 70 mg, green tea about 30 to 50 mg, dark chocolate roughly 20 mg per ounce, and pre-workout supplements can pack 200 to 400 mg per scoop. People often underestimate their total because they only count coffee. Add everything up as one custom dose in the timer for the honest picture.
